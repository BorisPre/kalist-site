The **handstand** is a skill of its own in calisthenics: strength matters, but it’s mostly about frequent practice and balance. The good news: you can train it with no equipment, just a wall and some space.

A safe **handstand progression for beginners** starts with your wrists, then teaches you to carry your weight on your hands, and to get up and **out** of the position safely. That’s the logic behind the six steps of the "Handstand" path in KALIST.

## Before you start

Pick a clear space with no furniture around and a stable, non-slip floor. A mat or cushion in front of you is a good idea for your first attempts.

Learn the **bail-out** on day one: if you tip over forwards, step one hand to the side and turn out, landing on your feet like a cartwheel. Knowing how to get out removes the fear, and fear is the biggest obstacle in balance work.

## The 6-step progression

::: step wrist-prep
Your wrists carry your whole body weight in a handstand. On all fours with palms flat, rock gently forwards, back and side to side, then add a few rotations. Two or three minutes before every session.
:::

::: step frog
Squat down, hands shoulder-width apart, knees resting on the backs of your arms. Lean forward slowly until your feet leave the floor. The frog stand teaches you to balance with your fingers: press your fingertips down to stop yourself tipping over.
:::

::: step pike-pushup
Hips high in an upside-down V, lower your head in front of your hands, then press back up. Pike push-ups strengthen your shoulders at an angle close to the handstand. The higher your feet, the closer you get to being upside down.
:::

::: step wall-handstand
Back to the wall: hands about 20 cm from the wall, kick up gently with one leg and rest your heels against the wall. Push the floor away with straight arms and squeeze your glutes. Come down one leg at a time.
:::

::: step chest-wall
Chest to the wall: start in a push-up position with your feet on the wall, then walk your feet up while walking your hands back, to a position you can control. This version fixes the banana back and builds a straight line: ribs in, glutes tight, shoulders open.
:::

::: step handstand
Kick up in the middle of the room and find your balance. Corrections come from your fingers (if you’re falling forwards) and the heel of your hand (if you’re falling back). At first a few seconds is plenty: do many short attempts rather than long sessions.
:::

## How to train

The handstand loves **frequency**: 10 to 15 minutes, four to six days a week, beats one long weekly session. Stop when the quality of your attempts drops.

| Block | Exercise | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | Wrists, shoulders, frog stand | 5 minutes |
| Line | Chest-to-wall handstand | 3 to 4 holds of 20 to 30 s |
| Balance | Freestanding attempts or wall drifts | 10 to 15 short attempts |
| Strength | Pike push-ups | 2 to 3 sets |

## Common mistakes

- **The banana back.** Arched lower back, ribs flared: balance becomes unstable. Work chest-to-wall with your ribs pulled in.
- **Bent arms.** Lock your elbows and push the floor away, shoulders by your ears.
- **Hands too close or fingers together.** Hands shoulder-width apart, fingers spread to correct better.
- **Going up without a way out.** Learn the bail-out before chasing long holds.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to learn a handstand?

Holding a few seconds freestanding often takes several weeks to several months of frequent practice. A stable 30-second handstand is a longer-term goal.

### Is the handstand dangerous?

Practised progressively, in a clear space and with a reliable bail-out, the risk is low. Avoid training near furniture or objects, and listen to your wrists.

### Handstand or headstand?

They’re different. In a headstand your head rests on the floor along with your forearms or hands. In a handstand, only your hands touch the floor, arms straight.
